Open questions

These are the questions this draft still needs answered by Matt, Joe and the team.

  1. 1

    The words we lead with. The guide proposes leading with what ClassE does rather than “diagnostic” or “intervention,” and treats intervention as a two-part answer. Does that work for you?

  2. 2

    Mastery on the report. The report says “mastery” off one question. What should the product call a correct answer, and a skill shown over time?

  3. 3

    Albert and text to speech. Which Albert features work in the US product today, and does text to speech?

  4. 4

    TEKS alignment. Which grades and subjects are aligned to the TEKS, so we can say “TEKS” in Texas?

  5. 5

    Curriculum setup. What does it take to set ClassE up on a new district’s curriculum, so we know what to promise?

02

Why this matters

The main purpose of this guide is not to give exact language, but to guide us in making language choices that accurately reflect our product and our beliefs, and that position us with US educators as aligned on what we all want: supporting students, helping teachers, improving outcomes and helping every student reach their full potential.

Where we propose specific language, there are often other options, and we may ultimately choose a different one.

US buyers are skeptical of edtech. Many have been burned before, so they look for signals, good or bad. They answer to their community for every choice they make with public money. A district that buys ClassE is saying, “This is the best place to spend public funds for our kids.” So we have to be above reproach.

What matters most is not the words. It is the mindsets behind them, and these beliefs are ours. This guide helps us say them the way US educators say them.

Most of the lines under “Sounds like” come from our own calls, including some of Christopher’s. They work in Israel. A US buyer hears them differently, so we keep the idea and change the words.

  • Before a call, read everything up to the word list.
  • Before a deck or email goes out, check the word list.

Our language has to match what ClassE actually does. ClassE measures student progress, analyzes the data, and then does two things.

What our words have to describe

  1. 1

    It helps the teacher. The teacher gets whole-class, small-group and student-specific recommendations, such as targeted support. The dashboard shows each student’s current progress toward mastery of each objective or standard, precise to the question. This takes seconds or minutes, not hours of grading and spreadsheets. The teacher decides what to do.

  2. 2

    It helps every student directly. When a student misses a question, ClassE gives a second question on the same skill, written a different way (the twin question). Albert, our AI tutor, asks the student questions so the student works it out. Every student gets extra reps on what they missed.

  3. 3

    So intervention is a two-part answer. With Albert on, ClassE delivers some of the targeted support itself. With Albert off, it helps the teacher intervene: it shows where each student is, groups students, and gives the teacher recommendations. Where a district limits student-facing AI, the second question still works without Albert.

03

The short version

Our core beliefs

  1. 1

    Every child can, and will, grow.

    Every child can make radical growth in the right conditions. Our job is to help build those conditions.

  2. 2

    Kids do well if they can.

    This is an idea from Ross Greene, a US psychologist. A student who is struggling is missing a skill, not the will. When a student has a learning gap, it means we have not found the right way yet. Never, ever because the kid does not want to.

  3. 3

    We never blame students or families.

    Nothing we say can be heard as blaming kids or not believing in them. We never blame parents. Families are our partners.

  4. 4

    Every student is an individual.

    We see each student as an individual. We never generalize about a child from a group or from one story. A student’s culture, language and community are strengths the student brings.

Every part of this guide reflects one or more of these beliefs.

Three things to remember

  1. 1

    “We help the student up to the question. We don’t bring the question down to the student.”

    “Help” says the student gets support to reach the question. School teams will always be able to modify and accommodate a student’s work.

  2. 2

    “The teacher leads. ClassE supports.”

  3. 3

    “We don’t have a controlled study on that yet. Here’s what we can show you.”

Words we use

These work well with US educators. Several came out of our September workshop.

  1. 1

    For what ClassE does: which students missed which standard, down to the question, recommended groups and next steps, extra reps, formative assessment data, progress toward mastery, targeted practice, personalized practice and feedback, the second question, data-informed, curriculum-aligned (where ClassE is set up for that curriculum), accessibility supports

  2. 2

    For what the teacher does: targeted instruction, small-group follow-up, reteach and reinforce, next steps

  3. 3

    For students: a student with a disability, English learner or multilingual learner, students who have not shown this skill yet

  4. 4

    For results: a correct response, score band, performance level

Always Use the buyer’s word first. Never correct how a buyer talks about its own students, and never repeat what one district says about itself to another.

Before the call

  • Check the state, and whether you are meeting a district, a charter network or one school. Then read “Texas and New York, side by side.”

  • If a report on screen says “mastery,” say what it shows: “These students got this question right.”

  • Before any conversation with a district, check its student-facing AI policies and local laws. That decides how much Albert becomes a selling point. Do not mention text to speech until the team confirms it works in the US product.

  • If teachers are in the meeting, talk to them, not about them: “You see which students missed which skill. You decide what to do next.”

If a word goes wrong in the call

  • “What do you call this in your schools?”

  • “I may be missing local context. How does this work in your schools?”

  • “Let me say that more carefully.”

  • “I don’t know. I’ll find out and get back to you.”

If a buyer asks

  1. 1

    “So you’re like i-Ready?” “Some parts are similar. Let’s show you what happens after a student misses a question.”

  2. 2

    About progress monitoring: It is the district’s process, tied to IEP goals and tiered support. Say what ClassE shows: “which questions each student got right and wrong, on which skill, this week.”

  3. 3

    About English learners, IEPs and advanced students: These are three separate questions. Ask: “What supports does your district already use for each?”

  4. 4

    About the AI: Do not say the AI explains or teaches. Say what the student sees: “The student gets a second question on the same skill.”

  5. 5

    About a product they already pay for: Do not offer to replace it. Ask: “What do your teachers use it for today?”

Appendix

For the product team

Some of these words are on the screen, and a rep can’t talk around them in a demo.

  • The report says “mastery” off one question. Decide what it calls a correct answer, and what it calls a skill shown over time.
  • If the product labels students with words like “needs help” or “competent,” describe what the student did instead.

US terms

504 plan
A plan under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act for a student with a disability who needs accommodations.
ENL
English as a New Language, the New York program name.
ESSA
Every Student Succeeds Act, which sets the evidence tiers.
FERPA
Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act, on student records.
IDEA
Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, the special education law.
IEP
Individualized Education Program.
MTSS
Multi-Tiered System of Supports.
STAAR
The Texas state test.
TEKS
Texas Essential Knowledge and Skills, the Texas standards.
WCAG
Web Content Accessibility Guidelines.
