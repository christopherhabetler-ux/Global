# ClassE US language guide

Prepared by Christopher Habetler with EdTech Expertz, for Matt Campbell, Joe Gelb and the ClassE team.

## Why this matters

Purpose: This guide flags the words a US educator can hear as outdated, controversial or unintentionally offensive, and gives the word to use instead, in every call, demo, deck and email.

Goal: US buyers hear a partner who shares their beliefs about students and teachers, and they trust ClassE with their students.

We know that US buyers are skeptical of edtech. Many have been burned before, so they look for signals, good or bad.

Results come first. But buyers also need to know we think the way they do. They answer to their community for every choice they make with public money, and a vendor they choose becomes part of that choice. So they look for a partner that works from the same principles.

A district that buys ClassE is saying, "This is the best place to spend public funds for our kids." So we have to be above reproach.

What matters most is not the words. It is the mindsets behind them, and these beliefs are ours. This guide helps us say them the way US educators say them.

Most of the Not lines in this guide come from our own calls, including some of Christopher's. They work in Israel. A US buyer hears them differently, so we keep the idea and change the words.

Use this guide in three ways:
- Before a call or demo, read "The short version."
- Before a deck, one-pager or email goes out, check any word you are unsure of in the word list.
- During a call, if a word goes wrong, use one of the lines in "If a word goes wrong in the call."

You will not remember everything. If you read one part, read the short version.

## The short version

### Our core beliefs

1. **Every child can, and will, grow.** Every child can make radical growth in the right conditions. Our job is to help build those conditions.
2. **Kids do well if they can.** This is an idea from Ross Greene, a US psychologist. A student who is struggling is missing a skill, not the will. When a student has a learning gap, it means we have not found the right way yet. Never, ever because the kid does not want to.
3. **We never blame students or families.** Nothing we say can be heard as blaming kids or not believing in them. We never blame parents. Families are our partners.
4. **Every student is an individual.** We see each student as an individual. We never generalize about a child from a group or from one story. A student's culture, language and community are strengths the student brings.

Every section in this guide reflects one or more of these beliefs.

### Three things to remember

- "We help the student up to the question. We don't bring the question down to the student."
- "The teacher leads. ClassE supports."
- "We don't have a study on that yet. Here's what we can show you."

### Seven kinds of words that land wrong

- **Words that label a child.** Describe what the student did. "Let's look at what each student did."
- **Words that are out of date.** Use today's word, and the buyer's word first. "English learner," not "ESL student."
- **Words that undercut teachers.** "The teacher leads. ClassE supports."
- **The district's words.** Tiers, IEPs, diagnosis, intervention and progress monitoring name what the district runs, never what ClassE is. "Your team decides which support each student gets. ClassE gives your team data."
- **Politically charged words.** Do not raise them. If the buyer uses one, follow their lead.
- **Claims we have not measured, and sales talk.** "We don't have a study on that yet. Here's what we can show you."
- **Words that sound translated.** "Practice," not "exercises." "Scores," not "marks."

### Before the call

- Check the state, and whether you are meeting a district, a charter network or one school. Then read "Texas and New York, side by side."
- If a report on screen says "mastery," say what it shows: "These students got this question right."
- Do not mention Albert or text to speech until the team confirms they work in the US product.

### If a word goes wrong in the call

- "What do you call this in your schools?"
- "I may be missing local context. How does this work in your schools?"
- "Let me say that more carefully."
- "I don't know. I'll find out and get back to you."

## The seven kinds, with examples from our calls

### 1. Words that label a child

We describe what each student did. We never rank students, and we never generalize from a group or from one story.

- Say: "Let's look at what each student did."
	Not: "It's very helpful to engage these kids."
	Why: The sentence is kind. "These kids" still turns individual students into a group.
- Say: "Seven students missed this skill. The teacher can teach them in a small group."
	Not: "Closing the gaps with the weakest students."
	Why: Closing the gaps is a good goal. "The weakest students" ranks children.
- Say: "This student missed these three questions. Here is what the teacher sees next."
	Not: "He's got competency issues across the board."
	Why: "Competency issues" labels the child. Describe the answers.

### 2. Words that are out of date

Some words that were standard a few years ago now sound careless in a US school. We let the school, network or district name things first, then we use the same word. We never correct how a buyer talks about its own students.

- Say, in Texas: "Your emergent bilingual students."
	Not: "Your ESL students."
	Why: ESL is still the program name in Texas, so it sounds right. It is not a name for the child.
- Say: "A student with a disability."
	Not: "We can help ADHD students."
	Why: In the US, most people name the student first and the disability second. Some autistic and Deaf people prefer to name the identity first. If a family uses its own word, use theirs.
- Say: The buyer's own word for its students and programs.
	Not: A correction of it.

Never repeat what one district says about itself to another district, such as "Closing the gaps, we were an F."

### 3. Words that undercut teachers

Nothing we say puts the software above the teacher, in place of the teacher, or in judgment of the teacher.

- Say: "The teacher leads. ClassE supports." Say it early in the meeting.
	Not: "The platform is teacher-proof."
	Why: In some countries this is a selling point. In a US school it says the vendor thinks the teacher is the problem.
- Say: "You can see which students missed which skill, so you know which students to teach in a small group and what to reteach."
	Not: "We want to save time for teachers, and not only save time, but make them better teachers."
	Why: It says the teachers are not good enough today.
- Say: "ClassE shows the teacher sooner which students missed which skill. The teacher decides what to do."
	Not: "ClassE finds gaps faster than teachers can."
	Why: The speed is real. But "than teachers" puts the software in a race with the teacher.

When teachers are in the room, talk to them, not about them: "You see which students missed which skill. You decide what to do next."

### 4. The district's words

Some words name what the district runs, never what ClassE is, even when they would be accurate. Tiers, IEPs, 504 plans, intervention, progress monitoring and diagnosis belong to the district. So do "mastery" and "proficient" as results.

- Say: "Your team decides which support each student gets. ClassE gives your team data."
	Not: "We are doing exercises plus a Tier 1 type of remediation."
	Why: Tiers are the district's system. A vendor does not place itself in one, even if the description fits.
- Say: "The student got these questions wrong on this skill. This is where we focus next."
	Not: "We can do 10 exercises and diagnose exactly what the problem is."
	Why: "Diagnose" sounds like a doctor. Only an evaluation, with the parent's consent, decides whether a student has a disability. ClassE never stands between a child and an evaluation.
- Say: "The student got these questions right on this skill."
	Not: "80% of the class got that question right, so 80% has achieved mastery in that standard."
	Why: A US buyer hears "mastery" and "proficient" as results the state test gives. One question cannot carry them.
- Say, once the team confirms text to speech works in the US product: "ClassE has accessibility supports, like text to speech. Whether a support matches an accommodation in a student's IEP or 504 plan is for that team to decide."
	Not: "We could upload an IEP and use it to tweak how the system works."
	Why: An IEP is a confidential student record. The line also says ClassE carries out the plan. Only the IEP or 504 team decides accommodations.
- Say: "When a student misses a question, ClassE asks a second question on the same skill, written a different way. The second question shows the teacher whether the student misses the skill again."
	Not: "We are a diagnostic and remediation tool."
	Why: The first label decides which product the buyer compares ClassE to. Buyers who heard labels like this answered, "Oh, so you're i-Ready."
- Say, if a buyer asks "So you're like i-Ready?": "Some parts are similar. Let's show you what happens after a student misses a question. We help the student up to the question. We don't bring the question down to the student."

"Targeted practice" and "data-informed" are safe for ClassE. If the district says "remediation" first, use it too. Do not use several assessment words for ClassE at once. Name only what ClassE does in that district.

### 5. Politically charged words

In both states, do not bring up DEI or parental rights as a topic. If the buyer uses a word first, follow their lead. The rules differ by state, so read "Texas and New York, side by side" before the call.

- Say: What ClassE does for each student.
	Not: "An equity engine that dismantles traditional testing and builds belonging."
	Why: Never describe ClassE as doing equity work itself, in either state.

Our calls have not touched race. If it comes up, do not raise it, and never guess about a child from race, language, neighborhood or income.

### 6. Claims we have not measured, and sales talk

If ClassE has no study for it, we say so. Then we say what we can show instead.

- Say: "What we can show you is which questions your students got right and wrong, on which skills, this week, against your own curriculum. We don't have a study yet on whether it raises scores."
	Not: "A measurable increase in the summative scores and the ratings overall for the class and for the school."
- Say: "It takes grading tasks off the teacher's plate. We don't have a number yet for how much time it saves."
	Not: "It saves a lot of time for the teachers."
	Why: A principal may hear that the teacher will have nothing to do. Name the task instead.
- Say: "Schools in Israel use it. We don't have a controlled study of its effect on grades yet."
	Not: "Thousands of kids that use ClassE in Israel have better grades, better results."
	Why: It is not evidence that ClassE caused it, and a US buyer will ask how we know.
- Say: "The student got these questions wrong on this standard. The teacher can step in here, and ClassE gives the student more practice on it."
	Not: "We analyze each answer and identify exactly where the student's reasoning is breaking down."
	Why: It claims to see inside the student's head. We can show which answers were right and wrong.
- Say, once the team confirms Albert works in the US product: "Albert, the AI tutor, asks the student questions, so the student works it out. It is one feature of ClassE."
	Not: "It's like 30 personalized tutors that know your students and understand their mistakes."
	Why: No one can check it.
- Say: "We help the student up to the question. We don't bring the question down to the student."
	Not: "We bring the students to the test, not bring the questions down to the students."
	Why: "Test" sounds like teaching to the test. "Help" says the student gets support to reach the question. Without it, a special education or English learner specialist can hear "no supports." The line is about ClassE's own questions. IEP teams can still modify a student's work.

### 7. Words that sound translated

These are understood, but they mark the speaker as not from here.

- Say: "Practice," "practice questions."
	Not: "Exercises," "an exercise platform."
- Say: "Scores," "grades."
	Not: "Marks."

## Texas and New York, side by side

The same word can be safe in one state and risky in the other. Check the state before the call, and whether you are meeting a district, a charter network or one school.

| | Texas | New York and the Northeast |
|---|---|---|
| Law and policy | SB 12, a Texas law limiting DEI work in K-12 schools, in effect since September 1, 2025. | The Culturally Responsive-Sustaining Education Framework, New York State guidance since 2018. |
| Equity words | Do not raise equity, DEI or identity words. If the district uses one, follow its lead. | Many schools use equity, identity and asset framing words. Some have grown quieter about them since 2025. Follow the buyer's lead, and say what ClassE does for each student. Never claim ClassE is itself culturally responsive. |
| How to open | "ClassE gives students practice on the TEKS, once we set it up with you." | "ClassE gives students practice on your curriculum and your standards, once we set it up with you." |
| State test | STAAR. Use the name the district uses. Its top level is "Masters Grade Level," so Texas educators say "masters" as an official label. Never use "mastery" for a ClassE result. | The New York State tests. |
| English learners | "Emergent bilingual" for the student. "ESL" or "bilingual" for the program. | "Multilingual learners/English language learners (MLLs/ELLs)" for the student. "ENL" for the program. |

In New York, using a buyer's own words about equity is not the same as raising DEI.
