## Who the audience is, from the text alone

Reading it cold, the reader is a non-US sales team, working in English as a second language, about to talk with US district buyers. You can tell from the short sentences, the glossed terms ("reteach (teach the skill again)"), the acronym list, and "They work in Israel" framing. That matches the stated audience of Matt, Joe and the ClassE team.

It also writes to a second reader, a US expert checking credibility. That's where the Texas Education Code sections, HB 4545 and HB 1416, the ESSA history of "LEP" and the 2-d citations come from. Those lines are for Matt, not Joe's team. They add weight without adding use for the person in the call.

Matt's ask (workshop Next Steps 1) listed nine things.

| Matt asked for | In the guide? |
|---|---|
| Category / descriptor | No. It says what not to call ClassE, then offers "practice." That's a word, not a category. |
| Primary promise | No. The promise in the workshop ("improve measurable student outcomes") never appears. P5 mostly says what we can't claim. |
| Process | No. It bans "diagnosis" and "progress monitoring," two steps of the workshop cycle, and puts nothing in their place. |
| Differentiator | Yes. It has the second question and "help the student up to the question." |

| Preferred terminology | Yes. This is the strongest part. |

**Verdict: it does about four of the nine things well.** It's an excellent guardrail document ("how not to lose the room"). It isn't yet the positioning and messaging document Matt asked for. The scorecard's simulated test agrees on the guardrail side: 1 trap in 60 guided vs 43 in 60 for the control. The 77 guided misuses also show the gap: 34 were product facts the guide can't supply.

## Large usability improvements, in priority order

### 1. Surface the contradictions with Matt's own document (do this regardless)

The guide quietly overrules the workshop takeaways in at least these places:

| Workshop says | Guide says |
|---|---|
| "Learning diagnosis and intervention platform" (working category) | Don't call ClassE a diagnosis tool or an intervention platform |
| Cycle: Practice, Evidence, Diagnosis, ... Progress Monitoring | Don't say "diagnose" or "progress monitoring" for ClassE |
| "One learning activity with multiple instructional functions" (the full-cycle story) | Don't say "a formative assessment, a diagnostic insight and a remediation" |
| "Support any curriculum" | Don't say "any curriculum" |
| "Tier 1 support ... tools for Tier 2 and Tier 3 intervention" | No tier as a name for ClassE |
| "Acceleration (very positive framing)" | Acceleration only for the district's own program |
| "Exercise" (Matt's stated preference) | "Exercises" sounds foreign; say "practice" |
| "Save time for teachers" (Joe) | Don't say "It saves a lot of time for the teachers" |
| Mastery vs proficiency needs an official product decision | Neither word for a ClassE result, and no product decision stated |

The guide may be right on every one. But Matt and Joe will find these by reading, which feels like being corrected. They'd rather be asked. The scorecard says 5 departures went into a separate note. Check that the note covers all nine rows above, then put the table on page 2 and in the cover email as "Decisions we need from you."

### 2. Lead with what to say, not what to avoid

Right now, the first thing a salesperson meets is beliefs, then Say/Not pairs. There's no pitch they can say out loud. Add one page before the short version:

- **What ClassE is**: one sentence. Pick one after decision 1 above. A placeholder that fits the guide's rules: "ClassE gives students practice on your curriculum, and shows the teacher which students missed which skill."
- **What it promises**: stronger skills on your standards. Say it without the scores claim until the Texas measurement plan reports.
- **How it works**: the workshop cycle, renamed in guide-safe words: practice, second question, what the teacher sees, small group or reteach, and practice again.
- **Why it's different**: the second question, and "we help the student up to the question."
- **Albert**: one sentence, or an explicit "not in US pitches yet."

### 3. Make the Say lines sellable

Many Say lines are accurate but flat: "The student got these questions right on this skill." Under pressure in a call, reps fall back to what sounds confident, which is the Not line. For every "we can't say X," give a confident line the rep can say instead, backed by what ClassE can show (Israeli usage, teacher surveys, the Texas plan). Otherwise the guide trains the team to sound like it's hedging. "We don't have a study yet" and "I'll find out" appear throughout.

### 4. Split it into three pieces for three moments

It's 14 pages (per the scorecard), and Matt asked for "one concise document." Keep it as one file, but make it three clearly separate parts:

- **Field card (two pages)**: the "What we say" page, the short version, and "If a word goes wrong." This is what the team actually uses.
- **Word list (lookup)**: see 5.
- **Background for Matt**: beliefs, legal citations, sourcing, and the TX/NY detail.

### 5. Turn the reference into an A-to-Z word list

The tables are organized by concept (Concept | Say | Do not say). But people look things up by the word they're about to use: "Can I say mastery?" Reorganize it as one alphabetical list, with each row showing the word, a green/yellow/red status, what to say instead, and a short why. It's faster in a call, easier for a second-language reader, and Ramy can use it directly for UI strings.

### 6. Add a short section for the product (Ramy)

The workshop said these sessions should shape the UI. List the product strings that need a decision: "mastery" or "achieved mastery" on reports, "exercise" in the UI, any tier labels, and the "needs help / competent" student labels. The guide already bans these in speech. A rep can't talk around a word printed on the demo screen.

### 7. Move the legal detail out of the body

Keep every citation, but move them to the Background part. In the body, a Why line should be one plain sentence. "At risk is a legal category in Texas" is enough without the code section.

## What I would not change

- The "Why" lines. They're the best part for a second-language team, because they teach the reason and not just the rule.
- The framing that the Not lines come from our own calls, including yours. That keeps it from reading as a correction of Joe.
- The TX/NY table. It's the most practical thing in the guide.
