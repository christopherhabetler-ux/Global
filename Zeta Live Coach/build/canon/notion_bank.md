## 2
For folks who don't know it, the KIPP Foundation is the national arm. Strategy, support and oversight. But it doesn't have mandate authority. Twenty-eight independent regions, which you can think of as twenty-eight independent districts, and forty-eight high schools. Nobody had to say yes to what we were doing, so we had to use influence and the quality of our ideas to get them to want to say yes.
First thing we did was we made the change theirs. Every single region was part of our listening tours, part of our iterative feedback, part of our working groups, so that when we announced what the strategy was, I was looking at people in the eye, watching them nod. All 28 regions and all 48 high schools adopted the strategy. There was an alternate path, and no school chose that alternate path.
Second, we didn't go for all twenty-eight at once. We shrank the change. We let our stronger regions get early wins, and let that win sell the rest of the product. Because of the clarity and quality of our tools, we were able to stack early wins, and the early wins sold the future work. It built a positive flywheel.
Third, we kept our finger on the pulse. We paid attention to the micro signals, the small, not-so-small things that tell you whether people are on board with you or turning off.
The one I'd point to is academic health. We started with clarity, we wrote the academic procedures manual, we rolled out training making it as simple as we could, and then we built a push-button data analysis tool that took eight thousand rows of data and got a teacher, a student and a school an individualized, actionable report. The data went from present but obscured to right in front of them. We piloted it, and it was highly effective.
That year, 46% of ninth graders across all 48 high schools finished the quarter at a 3.0 or better. Up six points. Best quarter KIPP had ever had on that number, and twenty schools hit the goal, which was also the most ever.
We built it, we tested it, it worked, and we handed it off. Now the regions are running the play.
That's the exact type of work I love doing. Finding a problem, understanding it deeply, listening to stakeholders, and building something with legs, with momentum. Then rolling it out tailored to each person's specific role, in a way that keeps investment and builds investment.

## 3
I came into Excel Academy with a mandate for change, in a turnaround year. We had an entire new staff. We stopped growing for a year because things were in such rough shape. We literally had a hundred students. I think about it in three phases.
Phase one was stability. An all-new staff needed clear leadership. I was dean of students, forty percent of the staff reported to me, operations included, and I was the most experienced educator on the leadership team. So I took the consistency and clarity from my classroom, the routines, procedures and systems, and brought them to the entire school. I spent six days writing the playbook on how we were going to do everything, my culture manual and my operations manual. My boss at the time thought that was a crazy use of time. It became one of the most important things we did.
Phase two was the adults. We really focused on the five dysfunctions of a team, and built a strong, best-idea-wins culture. Then we created an academic-focused environment where it was cool to be smart, and rigor and engagement were the primary drivers.
Phase three was student ownership. Honor council, a student events committee, lots of student committees. The principal and I taught classes throughout the year. We built an orientation leader program that put students in formal positions to speak to other students in genuine ways. On the first day of school we had kids telling kids how overwhelmed they were at first, and that they'd even told their parents they didn't like the school.
Every orientation lesson was dual purpose. We were explicitly teaching investment, and in column B, reteaching and reinvesting in our procedures and systems. The eighth graders finished orientation by writing a personal essay on why they're here and why they choose to work hard, and each kid got a poster with a key quote from it, hung where the whole school could see it. Every field trip went from an afternoon with a permission slip to a lesson before on how we'd live our values there, and a debrief after.
Because we weren't spending our time on discipline anymore, and our classes were high-functioning, we could put that time into building student culture. And that grew into students owning the culture, peer to peer.
We took that school from 270th in the state to number one, and got National Charter School of the Year.
